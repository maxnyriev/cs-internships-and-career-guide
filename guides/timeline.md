# 4-Year Timeline

A map, not a rule. If you're behind, start where you are today. If you're taking a heavy course load or working a job, do less. That's fine. Every pace and count below is a suggestion, not a study result.

> **Started late or transferred?** Don't read this as a list of what you missed. Find your row in [Where are you?](README.md#where-are-you), then use the [6-week sprint](applying.md#behind-start-here). Come back here for the dates.

**The big date to know:** summer internships at big companies open about **July to September of the year before**, and many fill up by the fall. Handshake's data ([reported by Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/02/24/internship-jobs-competitive-handshake-study-advice-gen-z/)) shows two big-company peaks: late August to September, and late January to early February. So an internship for the summer after junior year means applying in the fall of junior year, with a second chance in winter.

That is the early path, not the only one. Many roles are rolling, which means the company reviews applications as they arrive and hires until the spots are full. Some stay open into winter. Local employers often hire into spring. If you missed the fall, keep applying. **It's November 2026, so Summer 2027 postings are already open.**

**The market is tougher than a few years ago.** Postings are down and applications per posting are up ([the figures are in Start here](README.md#what-the-market-looks-like-so-you-can-plan-honestly)). Treat that as a reason to start early and widen your targets, not to quit.

## The internship ladder

**Your first internship target:** any internship by the end of sophomore year, even a small or local one. Then climb. In NACE's [Job Outlook 2026](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/candidate-selection/internship-experience-often-the-deciding-factor-between-equal-candidates), internship and industry experience had the most influence between equally qualified candidates, so each rung makes the next easier.

| Rung | Examples | What you get | Move up when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Local or small** | Regional bank, hospital IT, utility, manufacturer, campus IT | An easier yes, real tasks, a first resume line | You've shipped one thing and have a manager who'll vouch for you |
| **2. Mid-size or startup** | Regional software firms, defense contractors, growing startups | More engineers to learn from, bigger systems | You can explain your work in a 5-minute story and have numbers |
| **3. Big company** | Large tech, finance, and engineering employers | Brand, pay, structured programs | You've done rung 1 or 2, practiced coding, and have referrals |

You can skip rungs and apply to all three at once.

## Freshman year

**Fall**
- [ ] Go to class, do your own work, go to office hours.
- [ ] Join your ACM chapter or CS club.
- [ ] Early programs for first-years mostly close in the fall. Apply to one or two if you like, but they're long shots ([early programs](../EARLY.md)).

**Spring**
- [ ] Write your first [resume](resume.md).
- [ ] Build one small [project](projects.md), ideally with a friend.
- [ ] Apply to summer research programs (REUs) and campus jobs. REU deadlines are usually January to March, so look in the fall. (REUs need U.S. citizenship, permanent residency, or national status.)
- [ ] Easy coding start: solve one easy problem a week in the language from your class.

**Summer**
- Any job is a good summer. If you have spare time, build a project or take a free course.

**Done by the end of freshman year**

| Step | Done looks like |
|---|---|
| Go to class and office hours | A professor or TA knows your name, and you've asked at least three questions in office hours. |
| Join a CS club ([networking](networking.md)) | You've been to three meetings and know two upperclassmen well enough to ask a question. |
| Build one small [project](projects.md) | A repo a stranger can run by following the README, plus a one-line result ("Used by 12 classmates to split rent"). |
| Put it on [GitHub](github.md) | Your profile has your real name, and the project is pinned with a clear README. |
| Write a [resume](resume.md) | One page, saved as a PDF, reviewed by at least one person (career center, upperclassman, or professor). |
| Start your [wins file](README.md#start-a-wins-file) | One doc with every project, job, and result so far. |
| First summer | Any job counts, and it goes on your resume. Research or a finished project is great. |

## Sophomore year

**Fall**
- [ ] Take Data Structures seriously. Coding interviews lean on it heavily.
- [ ] Apply to sophomore programs and internships as early as you can (see [applying](applying.md)).
- [ ] Go to the career fair. Talk to local employers too.
- [ ] Volunteer to run one thing in a club.

**Spring**
- [ ] Keep applying. Smaller and local companies hire into spring, so this is your best window if you started late.
- [ ] Ask a professor about research.
- [ ] Build a bigger project with a team.
- [ ] Coding practice: 2 or 3 problems a week.

**Summer** (before junior year)
- [ ] Build up to about 5 timed problems a week (see below).
- [ ] Polish your resume and apply the week postings open.

**Done by the end of sophomore year**

| Step | Done looks like |
|---|---|
| Data Structures | You can explain and code a hash map lookup, a stack, and a breadth-first search without notes. |
| Bigger team [project](projects.md) | Two or more people committed code, it runs or is live, and you can name one number (users, speed, data size). |
| Coding practice ([plan](#coding-interview-practice)) | 2-3 problems a week, every week, from one list. |
| [Apply](applying.md) | Applications tracked in a spreadsheet, including local and mid-size companies, plus one career fair where you talked to five employers. |
| [LinkedIn](linkedin.md) | Profile matches your resume, with a photo and your project links. |
| Research or campus job | You asked one professor about research, or you hold a campus job you can describe in one bullet. |
| Second summer | An internship of any size, or research, or a job plus one finished project. |

## Junior year

- **Fall:** Apply as early as you can, from July on. Roles at big companies fill, but many stay open for months. This internship often leads to a full-time offer. Round two is January to early February.
- **Spring:** Interviews and offers. If nothing comes through, plan a summer of research, a serious project, or a local job. That still counts, and you can apply again in the fall.
- **Government, defense, and labs:** these often open in the fall for the next summer, in part because background checks and clearances take months. Federal student hiring was disrupted in 2025 (see [tracks](tracks.md#government-and-defense) for sources), so apply, but don't make it your only plan.

## Senior year

- **Fall:** Apply for full-time jobs (many open in late summer), or accept a return offer. [Simplify's new-grad list](https://github.com/SimplifyJobs/New-Grad-Positions) is updated daily. Thinking about grad school? Ask professors for letters early in the fall.
- **Spring:** Smaller companies are still hiring. Keep applying until you sign.
- **Offer deadlines:** an offer comes with a reply-by date. If you need more time, ask in writing and say why. See [offers](applying.md#offers).
- **Finish:** Decide, finish your degree, and thank the people who helped. Then help the students behind you.

## Coding interview practice

Most software internship applications include an online coding test or interview. Start light and build up. This pacing is a suggestion:

| When | Problems a week |
|---|---|
| Freshman year | 1 easy one a week, to get used to it |
| Sophomore year (after or during Data Structures) | 2 or 3 |
| The 8 to 10 weeks before the fall you apply | Build to about 5, with a timer |

How to do it:
- Learn the patterns: arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, recursion, trees, graphs.
- Use one language you know well (Java, Python, or C++). The examples in these guides use Java.
- Pick **one** list and finish it, such as [NeetCode](https://neetcode.io)'s 150 problems, and practice on [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com). [Grind 75](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/grind75/) is a shorter, week-by-week plan.
- Set a 30-minute timer. If you're stuck at 20 minutes, read the solution, then redo the problem from scratch the next day.
- Practice explaining out loud as you code.
- [ICPC](https://icpc.global) practice and contests count as practice too.
- Try a problem first, then compare it to a clean Java version. The reference repos are in [interviews](interviews.md#resources).
- Skip the giant "study for 6 months" plans like [coding-interview-university](https://github.com/jwasham/coding-interview-university). It's huge, and most interns don't need it.

**Sample 8-week ramp** (about 3 hours a week): weeks 1-2 arrays and hash maps, weeks 3-4 two pointers and strings, weeks 5-6 trees and recursion, weeks 7-8 graphs and one timed mock. Each week: 3 problems untimed, 1 timed.

**Done looks like:** you can solve a new easy problem in 15 to 20 minutes and a medium one in 30 while explaining out loud.

This plan is for software roles. Hardware, embedded, and security interviews ask different things, so see [tracks](tracks.md) before you spend weeks on LeetCode.

## Return offers

A return offer is an invitation to come back, for another internship or full-time. NACE's 2025 report put the offer rate for 2024 interns at [62%, the lowest in five years](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/internships/intern-offer-and-conversion-rates-fall-acceptances-rise), and its 2026 report put the conversion rate for 2024-25 interns at [63.1%](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/internships/intern-conversion-rate-hits-highest-mark-in-five-years) (employer surveys, all fields). So roughly 6 in 10: the internship is the best route to a job, not a guarantee. NACE's student survey also linked conversion to things you can influence: feeling supported by a supervisor, doing complex work, and belonging on the team (associations, not proven causes).

Most of the work happens during the internship:

- [ ] **Week 1: ask what conversion takes.** "What does someone do here to earn a return offer?" Write the answer down and ask again at the midpoint. Ask who decides and when.
- [ ] **Weekly:** a short update to your manager ("shipped X, working on Y, blocked on Z"), and a line in your [wins file](README.md#start-a-wins-file).
- [ ] **Midpoint feedback:** "What should I do more of, and what should I fix?" Then act on it and say what you changed.
- [ ] **Ship one scoped thing.** Agree with your manager on a small piece you can finish before week 8 (a test suite, a script, one feature). Finish it, demo it to the team, and write a one-page handoff so it outlives you.
- [ ] **Last two weeks:** "I'd love to come back. Is that possible, and what are the next steps?" Get their answer and the timeline in writing.

If there's no offer, ask for feedback and a reference, and keep in touch.

## Co-ops

A co-op is a longer work term, often a full semester, and some schools have a formal co-op program that you sign up for through the career center. Co-ops can repeat, so you may work for the same company more than once. Ask your career center how co-ops work at your school and how they affect your graduation date.
