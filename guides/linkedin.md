# LinkedIn

LinkedIn is where recruiters and professionals find each other. It matters less than your classes and projects, so spend about an hour setting it up, then about 15 minutes a week keeping it alive. The case for doing it at all: a 2022 study in *Science* of about 20 million LinkedIn users ([paper abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/)) found that moderately weak ties, like acquaintances and alumni you share a few connections with, were the most useful for job moves. A findable profile is how those people find you and how you find them.

*Everything about connection limits below may change. Check what the LinkedIn app currently shows you.*

## Profile checklist

- [ ] **Photo.** Face in good light, plain background, shoulders up. A phone photo is fine.
- [ ] **Name.** The same name as on your resume. If people often mispronounce it, add a short note in About, or use LinkedIn's name pronunciation recording if your app shows it.
- [ ] **Location.** Your real location (the city or area where you live or go to school). Recruiters filter by it. If you want to work elsewhere, say so in About.
- [ ] **Custom URL.** Change it to `linkedin.com/in/yourname` in settings.
- [ ] **Visibility.** If you're not ready to be found yet, check your profile visibility settings and make the profile public once your headline, projects, and photo are done.

## Headline

The line under your name. If you leave it blank, LinkedIn shows only your school or "Student".

**Before:** `Student at Example State University`

**After:** `CS student at Example State | Java, Python, SQL | Built a shift-swap web app`

Pattern:

> [Year] [Major] at [School] | [2-3 skills you've used] | [one thing you built]

Add "Seeking Summer [year] internship" once you're applying.

## Example profile

This is a made-up sophomore. Copy the shape, not the words.

**Headline:** CS student at Example State | Java, Python, SQL | Built a shift-swap web app

**About:**

> I'm a sophomore studying computer science at Example State. I like building small tools that fix everyday problems. Last semester I built a shift-swap board that 9 coworkers use instead of a group text. I also work part-time at a hardware store, where I learned to stay calm during a rush. I'm looking for a software internship for next summer. Email: name@email.com

Five short sentences: who you are, what you build, a project with a number, one non-tech strength, and what you want (plus your email).

**Project entry:**

> **Shift Swap Board** (Java, Spring Boot, SQLite) — Built a web app where coworkers post and claim open shifts, used by 9 coworkers for 6 weeks, and fixed a double-claim bug with a database transaction. Code and demo: github.com/yourname/shift-swap

**Non-tech job entry:**

> **Cashier, Grocery Store** — Checked out up to 80 customers a shift and kept the line moving by calling for backup early. Trained 2 new hires.

Write bullets the same way as your resume: what you did, how you know it worked, and how you did it. See [resume: bullet formula](resume.md#bullet-formula-xyz). Never invent numbers. If you don't have one, describe the work in plain words. Keep your LinkedIn and resume consistent.

## Fill in the rest

- **Education:** school, major, expected graduation date.
- **Experience:** any job counts, even non-tech. One or two bullets each.
- **Projects:** each with a one-line description and the GitHub or live link.
- **Skills:** 5 to 10 you have really used. Ask a professor or classmate to endorse one or two.
- **Featured:** pin your best project link or a live demo near the top, so a visitor sees proof without scrolling. This is a suggestion, not a tested trick.
- **Clubs and leadership:** ACM, other clubs, events you organized.
- **Languages:** list them if you speak more than one.

## Find alumni

Alumni are your best contacts. Steps:

1. Search for your school and open its LinkedIn page.
2. Click **Alumni** (or **People**).
3. Filter by where they live, where they work, and what they studied. Try "software", "engineer", or a company you like.
4. Open 5 profiles. Pick people who graduated 3 to 8 years ago. They're recent enough to remember the same recruiting cycle.
5. Send a request. **Connection notes are rationed on a free account.** LinkedIn's own help pages don't agree with each other: its "Personalize invitations to connect" page says three personalized notes a month, while its "Invitation limit reached" page says five. Either way it's a tiny budget, and a note maxes out at 200 characters (Premium gets unlimited notes). So treat notes as something you spend deliberately: send the plain request, and once they accept, send your message in the chat. Save a note for someone you really want to reach, and check what your own app shows before you count on it.

Template that fits a short note (under about 200 characters once you fill it in):

> Hi [Name], I'm a [Year] CS student at [School]. I saw you went here too. Could I ask you one quick question about your work at [Company]?

Aim for about 5 requests a week. If they don't answer, send **one** nudge a week later ("Just checking in. No worries if you're busy.") and then stop. What to send once they accept, and how to follow up after a chat: [networking: alumni](networking.md#alumni).

After you apply to a company, it's fine to message an alum there with one line saying you applied and why. See [applying: referrals](applying.md#referrals).

## Job search settings

- **Open to Work.** In your profile, choose Open to work and set the roles, places, and start date you want. You pick who sees it. "Recruiters only" (LinkedIn says only people using its Recruiter product can see it) keeps the green frame off your photo. The public badge is a tradeoff: LinkedIn says members who show it are nearly twice as likely to get a message from a recruiter ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91577144/linkedin-open-to-work-badge-redesign) repeats the claim). That's LinkedIn's own figure, not an independent study, and I found no comparable number for recruiters-only. Some people feel the frame looks desperate. Either choice is fine. In an active search, public may get you more messages.
- **Job alerts.** Search "software engineer intern" with your location and save it as an alert. Add "new grad" later. Make one alert per kind of role. Alerts help you apply early.

## Keep it alive

- Once a month, add anything new: a project, a club role, a class you finished.
- Check your messages once a week. Reply to recruiters politely, even to say no. Be careful with anyone who asks you to pay or to move to a chat app right away ([job-scam red flags](applying.md#job-scam-red-flags)).
- Update your headline when your goal changes (internship, then new grad).

**Done looks like:** a profile with a photo, a headline in the pattern above, one project with a link, and your education and a job or club role filled in.
