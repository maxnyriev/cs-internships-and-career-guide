# Start Here

Short, practical guides for computer science and computer engineering students at any U.S. school, written for people who have never had an internship, including late starters, transfers, and students who work.

**You will not do all of this, and you don't need to.** Your classes come first. Pick one or two things a semester. Hours, counts, and paces in these guides are suggestions to adjust to your life; where a number comes from a study, it's linked.

Some classmates coded in high school or already have an internship. That's a head start, not a final score. Steady work beats it: one project a semester, a few applications a week in hiring season, and showing up to the same club and office hours until people know you.

## What the market looks like (so you can plan honestly)

Entry-level hiring is harder than it was a few years ago. Three numbers worth knowing:

- Internship postings on Handshake fell by more than 15% from 2023 to 2025, while applications per internship more than doubled ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/02/24/internship-jobs-competitive-handshake-study-advice-gen-z/)).
- 38% of 200-plus engineering leaders said their company had cut entry-level hiring in the past year ([CodePath survey, via Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/12/18/tech-hiring-slow-but-not-fully-stalled-exclusive-codepath-data-new-secret-to-land-tech-role/)).
- Between two equally qualified candidates, employers rank internship and industry experience highest, and general work experience and leadership above a 3.0+ GPA ([NACE Job Outlook 2026](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/candidate-selection/internship-experience-often-the-deciding-factor-between-equal-candidates), 183 employers).

So apply to more places than you think you need, start earlier than feels necessary, and don't spend all your effort on the most crowded postings. Those figures cover all students and all roles, not CS alone. They describe a market, not you.

**How to pick your one or two things** (20 minutes, once a semester): list everything you could do (resume, project, applications, practice, club role, research ask). Cross out anything due more than a semester away. Circle the one with the nearest hard deadline, like a posting window or career fair. That's #1. Circle one cheap habit that runs all semester, like weekly office hours. That's #2. Put both on your calendar as repeating blocks.

**Done looks like:** two goals on one sheet and two repeating blocks on your calendar.

<a id="the-5-hour-week"></a>**Working or commuting? Try the 5-hour week:** 1 hour of class help, 2 on one project, 1 on applications or your resume, 1 on people (a club meeting or one message). It's slower, and it still works. One layout:

```
Mon  30 min  Office hours or tutoring visit
Tue  60 min  Project block
Wed  30 min  Applications or resume
Thu  60 min  Project block
Fri  30 min  Wins file + one message to a person
Sun  30 min  Internship check + plan next week
```

That's 4 hours of blocks plus an hour of slack. If you skip a block, skip it. Don't double up the next day.

## How internships work

- An **internship** is a short paid job, usually 10-12 weeks in the summer. A **co-op** is longer, often a full semester of full-time work. A **new-grad role** is your first full-time job after graduation.
- Big companies hire summer interns almost a year ahead. Handshake's data (via Fortune) shows two big-company peaks: late August to September, and late January to early February. Mid-size and regional companies recruit year-round. It's November, so Summer 2027 postings are already open. Start this week.
- Government and defense roles often open early in the fall because of background checks. Federal student hiring was disrupted in 2025, so don't make it your only plan ([tracks](tracks.md#government-and-defense)).
- Many students finish freshman year without an internship. A campus job, research, or a project is a good first summer.
- Aim for **any** internship by the end of sophomore year, even a small or local one. Then climb: local, then mid-size, then big if you want ([how the ladder works](timeline.md#the-internship-ladder)).

Semester-by-semester steps, with what "done" looks like for years 1 and 2, are in the [4-year timeline](timeline.md).

## Use the job board to decide what to learn

Once a month, 30 minutes: copy the requirements from 20 postings on the [job board](../INTERNSHIPS.md) in the role you want. Tally what shows up (Java, Python, SQL, Git, Linux, REST, testing). Circle the top 3 you lack, pick the easiest, and build a 2-week mini-project with it ([projects](projects.md)).

**Done looks like:** a sheet with 20 rows and one skill circled with a start date.

## Start a wins file

Make one document today. List every project, job, club role, and result, with real numbers: users, hours saved, grade, contest rank, dollars raised, people served. Spend 10 minutes on it the same day each week, while you still remember the details.

| Date | What I did | Number | Proof (link) | Skill used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 2026 | Club treasurer: tracked a shared event budget | $1,800 budget, event came in $90 under | spreadsheet link | budgeting, spreadsheets |
| Oct 2026 | Fixed duplicate sign-ups in the club form | 14 duplicates removed | before/after screenshot | spreadsheet cleanup |

No number? Write what you did in plain words. Don't invent one. Your resume, LinkedIn, application answers, and interview stories all come from this file. When you're ready to write resume lines, turn the best rows into a [master bullet bank](resume.md#keep-a-master-points-file).

## Glossary

- **OA (online assessment):** a timed coding test you take online, often the first step after you apply.
- **Referral:** someone at the company submits your name or vouches for you. In [Ashby's data](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals) (38 million applications, 2021-2024, all roles), about 40% of referred candidates reached an interview versus about 3% of inbound applicants. That's an average across every kind of job, not a promise.
- **ATS (applicant tracking system):** the software companies use to collect and sort applications. People, not software, usually make the call, but knockout questions like location, graduation date, or clearance can filter you out automatically.
- **REU (Research Experiences for Undergraduates):** paid summer research programs at universities, funded by the National Science Foundation. Open to undergraduates who are U.S. citizens, permanent residents, or U.S. nationals; each site can add its own rules.
- **Return offer:** an offer to come back (for another internship or full-time) after your internship ends.
- **Handshake:** a job site many colleges use for student postings and career fairs.
- **Tech screen:** a first technical interview, often 45-60 minutes of coding with an engineer.
- **Clearance:** U.S. government approval to work with classified information. The employer starts it, it can take months, and it usually requires U.S. citizenship.
