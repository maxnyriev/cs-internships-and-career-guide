# Applying

For most students, the hard part isn't being good enough. It's applying early, applying in the right places, and getting a person to read your resume. This guide takes you from a finished [resume](resume.md) to interviews. Counts and paces below are suggestions to adjust, not research findings. For the interviews themselves, see [interviews](interviews.md).

## Timing

- **Earlier is better, and late is not too late.** Handshake's data ([reported by Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/02/24/internship-jobs-competitive-handshake-study-advice-gen-z/)) shows big-company peaks in late August to September and late January to early February. Mid-size and regional companies recruit year-round, and local employers, contractors, and startups often hire into spring. Dates by year: [4-year timeline](timeline.md).
- **It's November 2026, so Summer 2027 postings are already open.** Do your first batch this week.
- A vendor-run recruiter survey (anecdotal) said applying in the first few days after a posting goes up can help. A cheap way to act on that: set 2 LinkedIn job alerts ("software engineer intern" and "new grad software engineer") and check this [job board](../INTERNSHIPS.md) two fixed days a week in the fall, once a week otherwise.
- **Stale postings exist.** A posting that's still up may already be filled or paused. Don't read silence on an old posting as a verdict on you.

## Where to apply

Don't spend all your effort where thousands of people apply. Handshake reported applications per internship more than doubled from 2023 to 2025 while postings fell. I haven't seen data on local employers' applicant counts, so "fewer people apply there" is a reasonable bet, not a measured fact.

1. **Your school's career fair and Handshake.** Employers there came looking for students from your school. This is the main channel for most students. Go every semester, even as a freshman.
2. **Regional employers.** Banks, insurance companies, energy and utility companies, hospitals and healthcare systems, manufacturers, and government and defense contractors all hire CS and CE interns. Many never post on the big internship lists.
3. **Mid-size companies and startups.** Often fewer applicants, and you often do real work.
4. **Big tech.** Apply, but don't make it your whole plan.
5. **This [job board](../INTERNSHIPS.md)**, plus the lists under [Resources](#resources).

A first internship anywhere makes the next one much easier. Local, then mid-size, then big is a normal path.

**Build a 25-row target sheet (about 2 hours, once).** Three tabs: 10 local or regional employers, 10 mid-size, 5 big names. For each: careers-page link, whether it posts interns, the month it usually posts (fill in after you see it once), and a named person if you have one. Find local employers by searching "[your city] largest employers" plus each industry above, by filtering your school's Handshake employer list to CS or IT, and by asking two upperclassmen, "Where did you intern that I wouldn't find on a big list?" Search each careers page for "intern," "student," or "early career," and set a March reminder for the ones with nothing yet.

**Done looks like:** 25 rows, each with a careers link and a "checked on [date]."

## How many to send

- **Cold applications to big companies are long shots.** In [Ashby's 2021-2024 data](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals) (all roles, not just interns), about 3% of inbound applicants reached an interview and about 40% of referred candidates did. Those are averages, so use them as direction. Most applications get no answer, and that alone doesn't mean you're bad. But 40 or more with no replies at all points to your resume or targeting (see the [funnel check](#funnel-check)), not bad luck.
- **Referrals and local employers do better per application.** Ten applications with referrals can beat 100 cold ones.
- **[Tailor](resume.md#tailor-it) only your top 10 to 15 targets.** Send a solid general version to everyone else.
- A reasonable weekly goal is 10 applications and 3 referral asks (5 applications if you work 20+ hours a week).

## Weekly routine in season

Pick fixed blocks and protect them, like a class. One example for a student with about 10 free hours a week:

| Block | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| Apply | 2 to 3 hours | Send applications, update your tracker |
| Practice and build | 6 to 7 hours | [Coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice), your [project](projects.md) |
| Follow up | 30 minutes | Thank-you notes, referral follow-ups |

On the [5-hour week](README.md#the-5-hour-week), give applications one 60-minute batch a week (6 to 8 applications with your answer sheet open) and put referral asks in your "people" hour.

**Order inside the apply block:**

1. **Referral requests first.** Ask before you apply, so the referral can be attached.
2. **Hard deadlines next.** Anything closing this week.
3. **Everything else**, newest postings first.

**Late in the season** (January on), sweep older postings that are still open. Some are, and fewer people apply to them, but some are already filled, so don't count on them.

## Tracker and answer sheet

**Tracker.** One spreadsheet row per application:

| Company | Role | Link | Date applied | Referral | Version sent | Status | OA date | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example Bank | Software Intern | (link) | Sep 12 | Yes, alum | general | OA sent | Sep 18 | Do OA by Sep 18 |

Status values: applied, OA, interview, offer, rejected, no reply. After 3 weeks with no reply, mark it "no reply" and move on. Put a few counters at the top so the [funnel check](#funnel-check) takes a minute: applications sent, referrals, OAs, interviews, offers (spreadsheet `COUNTA` and `COUNTIF` do it).

**Answer sheet.** One document you copy from so each form takes minutes, not an hour:

```
NAME / EMAIL / PHONE / CITY, STATE
SCHOOL / DEGREE / MAJOR / EXPECTED GRADUATION (month, year) / GPA (if 3.0+)
LINKEDIN / GITHUB / PORTFOLIO
WORK AUTHORIZATION: [truthful answer, same every time]
CLEARANCE: [none / eligible / have]
LOCATION PREFERENCES / EARLIEST START / LATEST END
WHY THIS COMPANY (2 sentences): ...
A PROJECT (3-4 sentences: problem, your role, tools, result): ...
A CHALLENGE (one STAR story, 5 sentences): ...
```

Autofill is fine for repeated fields, but read every application before you submit. **Knockout questions** (location, graduation date, degree, clearance) are the one kind of filtering that really is automatic, so answer them carefully and truthfully. A cover letter is optional unless the posting asks for one; if it does, 4 short paragraphs (why this company, your best project, one more fit point, thanks) is plenty.

## Referrals

A referral means someone who works at the company submits your name. It's the best way to get a human to read your resume.

**What it does:** gets your resume a real look. **What it doesn't do:** skip the coding test or interviews, or save a weak resume.

**Who to ask:** alumni, upperclassmen who interned there, people from career fairs, clubs, or hackathons ([networking](networking.md)).

**When:** before you apply, ideally a week or two before. Many referral systems only work if your application isn't in yet.

**Two-step ask for someone you've never met.** Step 1 asks for advice, not a favor:

> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] CS student at [your school]. I saw you interned at [Company] on the [team]. I'm applying for [role] this fall and would value 15 minutes to hear what the process was like. I can work around your schedule. Thanks either way.

Step 2, after they reply:

> Thank you for the advice about [specific thing]. I read the [role] posting ([link]) and plan to apply by [date]. If you're comfortable referring me, my resume is attached. If not, no problem at all.

Or, if you'd rather do it in one message, include the job link, your resume, one specific thing you'd like to work on, and a one-line project with a result. If they say no or don't answer after one follow-up a week later, thank them and move on. Never ask someone to refer you to a role you haven't read.

## Funnel check

Applying is a funnel: applications, then replies, then online assessments (OAs), then interviews, then offers. Find the first stage where you're stuck and fix only that one. Fixing a later stage doesn't help if you never reach it.

Low rates are normal: inbound interview rates in the low single digits show up in the data above. What matters is zero at a stage after a fair number of tries.

| What you see | Most likely problem | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 40+ applications and no OAs or calls at all | Resume or targeting | Run the [resume audit](resume.md#resume-audit), get a human to review it, apply to more regional and mid-size employers, get referrals |
| You get OAs but no interviews | OA speed and accuracy | Timed [coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice) at the before-you-apply pace, under a timer |
| You get interviews but no offers | Interview skills | Practice out loud with [interviews](interviews.md), do mock interviews at your career center |

Run the check after every 40 applications or so, not after every rejection.

## Behind? Start here

For a junior, transfer, or anyone starting late with no internship, no projects, and little on the resume. Six weeks, about 10 to 12 hours a week. If you work a job, stretch it to 10 weeks.

| Week | Do this | Done looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write or fix your [resume](resume.md). Get it reviewed by your career center or an upperclassman. Start your tracker and answer sheet. | A one-page resume someone else has read |
| 2 | Pick one [project](projects.md) that real people will use (a club, a job, classmates). Start building. Start daily practice: one easy coding problem a day. | A repo with a README and a first working piece |
| 3 | Send 10 targeted applications, mostly local and mid-size employers. Ask 3 people for referrals. Go to any career fair or employer event. | 10 rows in your tracker, 3 referral requests sent |
| 4 | Ship a first version of the project to its users. Another 10 applications and 3 referral asks. | Someone other than you has used it |
| 5 | Add the project to your resume with a result ("used by 40 club members"). 10 more applications. One mock interview. | Updated resume, 30+ applications total |
| 6 | 10 more applications. Run the [funnel check](#funnel-check). | A clear next fix, and a plan for the next 6 weeks |

**Widen what counts:**

- **Spring, fall, and off-cycle internships.** Fewer applicants. Some schools let you take a semester off for these.
- **[Co-ops](timeline.md#co-ops).** Common at manufacturers, utilities, and defense companies.
- **Part-time roles** during the school year, including IT help desk and campus tech jobs.
- **Undergrad research** with a professor at your school.
- **Local employers** that never post on national lists. Look them up by industry in your city and check their careers pages directly.

## Online assessments

Many companies send a timed coding test right after you apply, often before a person looks at your resume.

- **Format:** often 60 to 90 minutes with 2 to 4 problems, on a platform like [HackerRank](https://www.hackerrank.com) or [CodeSignal](https://codesignal.com). Hidden test cases check your answer. Some add multiple-choice or debugging questions.
- **How to practice:** timed problems from the [coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice) list. Try each platform's free practice tests so the editor isn't new on test day. Read the whole problem and the examples before typing.
- **On the day:** quiet room, stable internet, a language you know well. Solve the easiest problem first. A brute-force solution that passes some tests beats a clever one that passes none.
- **AI tools:** rules differ by company, and some now allow AI in certain rounds. Read the OA instructions and ask the recruiter if unclear. Where AI is banned, some tests record your screen or webcam. Never submit code you can't explain; companies often ask about your OA code in the next round.

## Offers

- **Reply within a day.** That's courtesy, not a rule. Thank them and say when you'll decide, even if you need time.
- **Get the deadline in writing.** NACE says one to two weeks is a typical window and encourages employers to grant reasonable extensions. Ask: "Could you confirm the deadline for my decision by email?"
- **Ask for more time politely if you need it:**
  > Thank you so much for the offer. I'm very excited about it. I'm finishing a couple of other interview processes and would like to make a careful decision. Would it be possible to extend my deadline to [date]? Could you confirm the new date by email?
- **Use an offer to speed up others.** Tell companies you're still interviewing with:
  > I've received an offer with a deadline of [date]. [Company] is a strong interest of mine. Is there any way to move my timeline up?
- **Decline kindly:**
  > Thank you for the offer and for the time your team spent with me. I've decided to accept another opportunity. I'd like to stay in touch.
- **Reneging** (accepting, then backing out for another offer) is unfair to the company and other candidates, and NACE's guidance asks students to accept in good faith. It can also hurt your standing with that company and your career center (my judgment, not a published penalty). Don't accept until you're sure.
- **Pay:** I haven't seen data on how often intern pay is negotiated. It's fine to ask once about relocation or housing help. Full-time offers have more room.
- **After any interview,** send a short thank-you within 24 hours: "Thank you for talking with me today about [team]. I enjoyed hearing about [specific thing]. I'm still very interested. Please let me know if I can send anything else."

## Job-scam red flags

Scammers target students with fake internships and remote jobs. The FTC says honest employers never ask you to pay to get a job. Stop if you see any of these:

- **You're asked to pay** for anything: training, a starter kit, equipment, a background check.
- **They send a check** for equipment, then ask you to send some money back or buy gift cards. FTC: never bank on a "cleared" check.
- **The job is receiving and reshipping packages.** The FTC says that's never a real job.
- **Interviews happen only by text or a chat app**, with no video or phone call.
- **No company email.** The "recruiter" writes from a personal email address, and the company's real careers site doesn't list the job.
- **Pay that's too good** for little work, or an offer with no interview at all.
- **They ask for your Social Security number or bank details** before a real, written offer.
- **A "mentor" or "networking program" that charges you** for introductions or a guaranteed referral.

If you're unsure, find the company's careers page yourself (not through their link) and check that the job is there. Report scams at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

## Rejections

You will get many. Every person who got an internship also got rejections.

- **It's normal.** Most rejections are about timing and volume, not you.
- **Ask once for feedback** after an interview: "Thank you for letting me know. If you have any feedback that would help me improve, I'd appreciate it." Many companies can't share any. Don't ask again.
- **Log what stumped you.** Keep a list: the OA problem you couldn't finish, the question you fumbled. Practice those next. That list is your study plan.
